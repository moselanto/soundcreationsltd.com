### Body copy

**Sound isolation (soundproofing) stops sound travelling from one space to another**: from a church into the homes next door, from a club into a hotel room, or between two events running side by side. It is different from acoustic treatment, which controls echo *inside* a room. Most noise complaints need isolation, not more panels on the wall. Sound Creations designs and installs both, and we will tell you which one your problem actually needs.

#### Isolation or treatment: which do you need?

- **Neighbours can hear you, or you can hear them** - that is an isolation problem. Sound is escaping through walls, roofs, doors, windows, floors or ventilation.
- **The room itself sounds echoey, boomy or unclear** - that is a treatment problem. See [Acoustics](/solutions/acoustics/) and [Acoustic panels and ceilings](/solutions/acoustic-panels-ceilings/).

Acoustic foam and wall panels absorb sound inside a room, but they do very little to stop it passing through a wall. Many spaces need both, designed together.

#### How sound isolation is done

Sound finds the weakest path. Good isolation deals with every path, not only the obvious wall.

- **Mass.** Heavier, denser constructions block more sound. Adding layers of dense board or masonry raises the isolation of a wall or ceiling.
- **Decoupling.** Separating the two sides of a wall or ceiling (resilient mounts, independent stud frames, a room-within-a-room) stops vibration travelling straight through the structure. This matters most for bass from music and sound systems.
- **Sealing air gaps.** Even a small gap leaks a surprising amount of sound. Gaps around doors, service penetrations, cable routes and the tops of partitions must be sealed with suitable acoustic sealants and details.
- **Doors and windows.** These are usually the weakest elements. Acoustic doors with proper seals, lobbies (two doors in series) and laminated or double glazing make a large difference.
- **Floating floors.** Where impact noise or heavy bass reaches the rooms below (gyms, clubs, studios on upper floors), a floor built on resilient isolators breaks the path into the structure.
- **Ventilation and air-conditioning (HVAC) paths.** Ducts and open vents are direct sound paths. Lined ducts, silencers and acoustic louvres let air through while limiting sound.
- **Dividing doors and operable walls.** Large halls can be split into separate rooms with acoustic dividing doors, so several events can run at once.

#### Typical situations we see in Kenya

- **Churches close to homes.** Worship music and amplified sermons reaching neighbours, often through open windows, roof sheeting and gaps under eaves.
- **Clubs, bars and restaurants.** Noise complaints from residents or nearby hotel rooms, especially low-frequency bass at night.
- **Recording and rehearsal studios.** Keeping traffic and neighbours out and the music in, so recordings stay clean.
- **Offices and boardrooms.** Speech privacy between meeting rooms, executive offices and open-plan areas.
- **Hotels and event venues.** Quiet guest rooms near function halls, and halls that divide into smaller rooms.

### FAQs (paste into the FAQ field)

1. **What is the difference between soundproofing and acoustic treatment?**
   Soundproofing (sound isolation) stops sound passing between spaces, for example from a club to neighbouring homes. Acoustic treatment controls echo and reverberation inside a room so speech and music are clear. Panels and foam treat a room; they do not soundproof it. Many projects need both, designed together.

2. **Will acoustic foam or panels stop my neighbours hearing us?**
   Usually not. Foam and panels absorb sound inside the room but add little mass or separation to a wall. Stopping sound reaching neighbours needs isolation: heavier or decoupled constructions, sealed gaps, acoustic doors and windows, and treated ventilation paths.

3. **Why does bass still get through after soundproofing?**
   Low frequencies carry the most energy and travel through the building structure itself. Controlling them needs mass and decoupling, such as independent wall linings, resilient ceilings or floating floors, plus sealing of every air gap. A survey identifies which path the bass is using.

4. **Can a church be soundproofed from nearby homes?**
   In most cases noise can be reduced significantly. Common weak points in Kenyan churches are open windows, roof sheeting, eaves gaps and doors. A survey shows which paths matter most, so money goes on the measures that make the biggest difference.

5. **Can a large hall be divided into separate rooms that do not disturb each other?**
   Yes. Acoustic dividing doors or operable walls, combined with suitable ceilings and seals, let one hall run several events at once. Sound Creations did this at the Sarit Expo Centre in Westlands, Nairobi.

6. **How much does soundproofing cost in Kenya?**
   It depends on the noise level, which paths need treating, the construction and the target you need to reach. A wall upgrade is very different from a room-within-a-room. We quote after a site survey, so you pay for what your space actually needs.

7. **Do you work outside Nairobi?**
   Yes. Sound Creations works across Kenya and the region, from our Nairobi office at Mpaka Plaza, Westlands. Contact us with your location and the problem, and we will arrange a survey.

#### What a good conferencing system includes

- **Microphones that suit the room.** Ceiling microphones keep tables clear and cover the room evenly; table or boundary microphones suit smaller rooms and fixed seating; gooseneck microphones suit formal council or board layouts. The choice depends on room size, ceiling height and how people sit.

- **Room acoustics.** Hard glass walls, tiled floors and bare ceilings create reverberation that no microphone can fully fix. Treating the room is often the single biggest improvement in call quality. See [Acoustics](/solutions/acoustics/).

### The challenge

The sanctuary had poor acoustics for a long time, and earlier acoustic products the church tried had not solved it. It is a single-level, T-shaped building finished entirely in hard materials: a terrazzo floor, natural stone walls, and a "ceiling" that was simply the underside of corrugated iron roofing on steel trusses and purlins. Sound reflected around the space for a long time, destroying speech clarity.

The church set up a dedicated acoustic committee to find a durable solution. A committee member found Sound Creations through a thesis on acoustic performance in contemporary churches published on the company's website, and the company was selected in a competitive process after site tests, simulations and consultative meetings. Before committing, the committee visited the Seventh-day Adventist Church in Lavington, a previous Sound Creations acoustic project, and then arranged for about 120 congregants (two busloads) to visit as well.

### The measurements

Sound Creations measured reverberation time (RT60) in the sanctuary using acoustic test equipment:

| Frequency | Measured RT60 before | Recommended for the room volume |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Low (125 Hz) | up to 3.77 s | - |
| Mid (500-1,000 Hz, average) | 3.32 s | 1.22 s |

At more than three seconds, each spoken syllable was still ringing when the next began.

### The solution

Sound Creations recommended and installed about **740 m2 of Rockfon Artic** stone wool acoustic ceiling panels, suspended at least 200 mm below the existing roof and following its slope. Rockfon Artic combines high sound absorption with good light reflection and thermal insulation, so the ceiling also addressed brightness and heat under the iron roof.

### The result

Reverberation time was measured again after installation. Mid-frequency RT60 had fallen **from 3.3 seconds to 1.1 seconds**, matching the design prediction and coming in below the 1.22 s recommendation. The church also became brighter and cooler, with improved aesthetics. Committee members expressed great satisfaction with the result.

### Why it matters for other churches

Most Kenyan church echo problems come from the same hard finishes: tile or terrazzo floors, masonry walls and exposed roofs. Measuring first means the treatment is sized to a target rather than guessed, and verifying afterwards proves the result. See [Church and worship sound systems](/solutions/church-worship-sound-systems/), [Acoustics](/solutions/acoustics/), [Acoustic panels and ceilings](/solutions/acoustic-panels-ceilings/) and [Rockfon](/brands/rockfon/), and the related [PCEA Kahawa Farmers Church](/projects/pcea-kahawa-farmers/) project.
